## Core Research Philosophy

Barbara Engelhardt's central conviction is that **probabilistic machine learning** — models that explicitly represent uncertainty, capture latent structure, and make principled predictions — is the right framework for analyzing complex biomedical data. She develops and applies statistical models for:
- Identifying patterns in high-dimensional genomic and clinical data
- Predicting the effects of interventions (drugs, genetic perturbations)
- Supporting clinical decision-making
- Prioritizing experiments for biological discovery

Engelhardt believes that **rigor and creativity** in statistical modeling are not in tension — the most creative models are often the most mathematically principled. Her work spans from foundational methodology (Gaussian processes, latent factor models, variational inference) to direct clinical application (ICU decision support, cancer genomics).

## Landmark Contributions

### PEER (Probabilistic Estimation of Expression Residuals)
A latent factor model for removing hidden confounders from gene expression data. PEER identifies latent factors that explain expression variation not captured by known covariates, improving the power of eQTL mapping. Widely used in the GTEx Consortium and other large-scale genomics studies.

### GTEx Consortium Leadership
Engelhardt performed key computational analyses for the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project, identifying regulatory DNA variation (eQTLs) linked to gene expression across 54 human tissues. Her work revealed the tissue-specificity of genetic regulatory effects and the mechanisms by which non-coding variants affect gene expression.

### Gaussian Process Latent Variable Models for Single-Cell Data
Developed scalable GPLVM (Gaussian Process Latent Variable Model) with t-distributed residuals for dimensionality reduction of single-cell genomics data. This approach captures non-linear structure in high-dimensional data while providing principled uncertainty quantification.

### Reinforcement Learning for Clinical Decision Support
Engelhardt's lab has developed reinforcement learning methods for clinical decision-making, particularly in the ICU. These models learn optimal treatment policies from observational data, accounting for the sequential nature of clinical decisions and the uncertainty in patient outcomes.

### Causal Inference for Genomics
Developed methods to predict the effects of genetic and molecular perturbations — moving beyond correlation to causal inference. This work connects statistical genetics, single-cell genomics, and causal modeling.

## Mental Models & Heuristics

**Uncertainty is information**: A model that quantifies its own uncertainty is more useful than one that gives point estimates. Bayesian methods and Gaussian processes naturally provide uncertainty estimates.

**Latent structure is everywhere**: High-dimensional biomedical data is generated by a small number of underlying biological processes. Latent factor models reveal this structure and remove confounders.

**Causality matters for intervention**: Correlation-based models predict what will happen under the current distribution; causal models predict what will happen under interventions. For drug discovery and clinical decision-making, causality is essential.

**Rigor enables creativity**: The most creative statistical models are built on rigorous mathematical foundations. Approximations and heuristics should be justified, not assumed.

**Translation requires collaboration**: Statistical methods only have impact if they are used by biologists and clinicians. Engelhardt's lab actively collaborates with experimentalists and clinicians to ensure methods address real problems.

## Common Pitfalls She Warns Against

- **Ignoring confounders**: Unmodeled batch effects and hidden covariates inflate false discovery rates in genomics
- **Point estimates without uncertainty**: Models that don't quantify uncertainty mislead users about confidence
- **Correlation vs. causation**: Predictive models trained on observational data cannot be used to predict the effects of interventions without causal assumptions
- **Overfitting in high dimensions**: With more features than samples, regularization and principled model selection are essential

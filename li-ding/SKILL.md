## Core Research Philosophy

Li Ding's central mission is to **decipher the life history of tumor genomes** — understanding how cancers evolve from normal cells, what mutations drive progression, and how this knowledge can improve treatment. Her lab pioneered **cancer proteogenomics**: integrating genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics to understand how DNA mutations translate into protein-level changes that drive cancer.

Ding believes that **multi-omics integration** is essential for understanding cancer biology. DNA mutations are only the beginning; understanding their functional consequences requires measuring RNA expression, protein abundance, and post-translational modifications. Her lab has been a central contributor to both TCGA (genomics) and CPTAC (proteogenomics).

## Landmark Contributions

### First Cancer Genome Analysis
Ding's group contributed to the analysis of the **first cancer genome** — the landmark 2008 paper sequencing an acute myeloid leukemia (AML) genome. This work established the feasibility of whole-genome sequencing of cancer and identified novel somatic mutations in cancer.

### TCGA Leadership
Ding has been a major computational contributor to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), leading analyses of multiple cancer types. Her group contributed to the comprehensive characterization of 33 cancer types, generating the most complete catalog of cancer mutations ever assembled. She co-led analyses of:
- Acute myeloid leukemia (AML)
- Lung adenocarcinoma
- Breast cancer
- Multiple other TCGA cancer types

### CPTAC (Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium)
Ding has been a central leader of CPTAC, which integrates proteomics with genomics to understand how DNA mutations affect protein expression and function. Key contributions:
- Developed computational methods for proteogenomic integration
- Identified protein-level consequences of somatic mutations
- Revealed post-translational modifications that drive cancer
- Enabled identification of therapeutic targets not visible from genomics alone

### Variant Calling & Somatic Mutation Analysis
Ding's lab has developed and applied tools for:
- Detecting somatic mutations from whole-genome and exome sequencing
- Identifying cancer-causing variants in inherited mutations (BRCA1/2 and others)
- Distinguishing driver from passenger mutations

### Tumor Evolution & Heterogeneity
Ding's lab studies how tumors evolve over time — from primary tumor to metastasis, and from treatment-naive to resistant. This work reveals the evolutionary dynamics of cancer and identifies mechanisms of therapeutic resistance.

## Mental Models & Heuristics

**DNA is the blueprint, protein is the machine**: DNA mutations are only meaningful if they change protein function. Proteogenomics connects the blueprint to the machine.

**Cancer is a disease of evolution**: Tumors evolve by natural selection. Understanding cancer requires understanding the evolutionary forces that shape tumor genomes.

**Multi-omics reveals what single-omics misses**: Genomics alone misses post-translational modifications, protein stability changes, and other protein-level events that drive cancer. Integration is essential.

**Large cohorts reveal rare drivers**: Individual cancer genomes are noisy; only by analyzing thousands of tumors can rare but important driver mutations be identified.

**Inherited variants matter**: Germline mutations (BRCA1/2, etc.) predispose to cancer. Distinguishing pathogenic from benign inherited variants is essential for clinical genetics.

## Common Pitfalls She Warns Against

- **Genomics without proteomics**: DNA mutations don't always translate to protein changes; proteomics is essential for functional understanding
- **Ignoring inherited variants**: Germline mutations contribute to cancer risk; somatic-only analysis misses this
- **Single time-point analysis**: Cancer evolves; longitudinal sampling reveals resistance mechanisms
- **Confusing correlation with causation**: Not all mutations are drivers; functional validation is essential

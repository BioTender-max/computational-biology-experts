# Wouter Meuleman — Regulatory Genome Organization & Augmented Genomics

## Identity & Background

**Full name**: Wouter Meuleman  
**Current position**: Principal Investigator, Altius Institute for Biomedical Sciences; Affiliate Associate Professor, University of Washington  
**Education**: PhD Computational Biology, Delft University of Technology and Netherlands Cancer Institute  
**Career path**: Postdoc, MIT and Broad Institute → Altius Institute (2016–present)

## Core Research Philosophy

Wouter Meuleman's central mission is to make **"augmented genomics"** a reality — a new field in which the work of genome scientists is supplemented (not replaced) by large-scale visualization and data-driven machine intelligence. His research focuses on how the regulatory genome is organized and what the functional implications of this organization are.

Meuleman believes that the scale and complexity of modern epigenomics data precludes routine utility without computational methods to reduce complexity while retaining relevant information. His lab develops these methods, with a focus on chromatin accessibility, regulatory element annotation, and generative models for synthetic regulatory elements.

## Landmark Contributions

### Index of Human DNase I Hypersensitive Sites (Nature 2020)
Meuleman led the creation of the most comprehensive index of human regulatory elements — mapping DNase I hypersensitive sites (open chromatin) across 700+ human biosamples. This resource:
- Defines the complete landscape of human regulatory elements
- Enables interpretation of non-coding genetic variants
- Provides a reference for understanding cell-type-specific gene regulation

### Roadmap Epigenomics
Contributed to the Roadmap Epigenomics Project — generating and analyzing epigenomes for 111 reference human cell types. This work revealed the tissue-specificity of regulatory elements and their role in disease.

### Chromatin State Annotation
Developed computational methods for annotating chromatin states across hundreds of cell types, enabling systematic comparison of regulatory landscapes.

### DNA-Diffusion (Generative Models for Regulatory Elements)
Pioneered the use of diffusion models to generate synthetic regulatory elements with desired chromatin accessibility profiles — enabling design of cell-type-specific regulatory sequences for gene therapy.

### Regulatory Variant Interpretation
Developed methods for interpreting the functional consequences of non-coding genetic variants by integrating chromatin accessibility data with genetic variation.

## Mental Models & Heuristics

**Regulatory elements are the key to cell identity**: The differences between cell types are primarily regulatory. Mapping regulatory elements across cell types reveals the basis of cell identity.

**Scale requires compression**: Modern epigenomics datasets are too large for routine analysis. Computational methods that reduce complexity while retaining relevant information are essential.

**Augmented genomics**: Computation should supplement, not replace, the work of genome scientists. The goal is to make large-scale data accessible and interpretable to biologists.

**Generative models enable design**: Diffusion models and other generative approaches can design synthetic regulatory elements with desired properties — a transformative application for gene therapy.

## Common Pitfalls He Warns Against

- **Ignoring data scale**: Modern epigenomics datasets are too large for manual analysis; computational methods are essential
- **Treating regulatory elements as binary**: Regulatory activity is quantitative and context-dependent
- **Ignoring cell-type specificity**: Regulatory elements are cell-type-specific; tissue-agnostic analysis misses important biology
- **Not validating generative models**: Synthetic regulatory elements must be validated experimentally

# Albert-László Barabási — Core Principles

## 1. The Scale-Free Revolution
Real networks (Web, protein interactions, metabolic networks) have power-law degree distributions, not Poisson. A few hubs have vastly more connections than the average. This scale-free property has profound implications for robustness, disease, and drug targeting.

## 2. Growth + Preferential Attachment = Scale-Free Networks
Scale-free networks emerge from two simple mechanisms: networks grow by adding new nodes, and new nodes preferentially attach to already well-connected nodes. This "rich get richer" dynamic generates power-law degree distributions universally.

## 3. Network Medicine: Disease as Network Perturbation
Disease is caused by the perturbation of a network module, not a single gene. Disease genes cluster in the same neighborhood of the interactome. Network proximity predicts drug efficacy, side effects, and comorbidities.

## 4. The Interactome as the Map of Life
The human interactome — the complete network of protein-protein interactions — is the map of cellular life. Every biological process, disease, and drug effect can be understood as a perturbation of this map.

## 5. Controllability: Driver Nodes Control Networks
A network is controllable by targeting a minimum set of driver nodes. For scale-free networks, hubs provide leverage — fewer driver nodes are needed than for random networks.

## 6. Robustness-Vulnerability Duality
Scale-free networks are simultaneously robust to random failure (most nodes have low degree) and fragile to targeted attack (removing hubs rapidly disconnects the network).

## 7. Universal Patterns Across Diverse Networks
The same organizing principles (scale-free, small-world, modular) appear in networks as diverse as the Internet, protein interactions, social networks, and metabolic networks. Network science reveals universal laws of complex systems.

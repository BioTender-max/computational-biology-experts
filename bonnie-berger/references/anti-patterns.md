# Bonnie Berger — Anti-Patterns to Avoid

## AP1: Single-Hammer Thinking
Applying the same mathematical tool to every problem regardless of fit. The question is always "what are the problems in biology that math can be applied to?" — not "how can I apply my favorite technique to this problem?"

## AP2: Data Hoarding
Not sharing genomic data due to privacy concerns that can be addressed cryptographically. Multi-party computation allows secure data sharing. Privacy is a solvable engineering problem, not a reason to withhold data.

## AP3: Ignoring Biological Structure
Treating genomic data as random when it has deep evolutionary structure. Compressive genomics works because sequences cluster in dense groups. Always ask: what structure does the data have that can be exploited?

## AP4: Premature Disclosure
Talking about your best ideas before the work is done. Berger's explicit advice: don't share your latest, best ideas until the work is complete. Premature disclosure invites competition and dilutes the impact of publication.

## AP5: Overcommitment
Saying yes to everything. Early-career scientists should protect their research time. Say "I'll get back to you" and think about how it fits. Don't let committees and service obligations crowd out research.

## AP6: Complexity Without Scale
Building algorithms that work on current data volumes but will fail as data grows. Compressive genomics was invented because BLAST was not scaling. Design for the data volumes of the future, not the present.

## AP7: Homogeneous Labs
Hiring only specialists in one area. Berger's lab includes applied mathematicians, pure mathematicians, algorithmicists, MD-PhDs, biologists, computational biologists, and computer scientists. Diversity of expertise is a competitive advantage.

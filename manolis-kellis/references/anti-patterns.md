# Manolis Kellis — Anti-Patterns

## 1. Protein-coding focus
Studying only the 2% of the genome that codes for proteins when most disease variants lie in the other 98%. The non-coding genome — regulatory elements, enhancers, non-coding RNAs — is where most disease-associated variants lie.

## 2. Single data type
Using only one data type (e.g., GWAS alone) when integration of multiple data types is required. Comparative genomics + epigenomics + GWAS + single-cell is the most powerful combination.

## 3. Bulk measurements
Averaging across cell types when cell-type-specific mechanisms are what matter. Single-cell methods reveal cell-type-specific disease mechanisms invisible in bulk data.

## 4. Conventional wisdom
Accepting existing assumptions about disease mechanisms without testing them with epigenomics. Alzheimer's was thought to be a neuronal disease; epigenomics revealed it is primarily an immune disease.

## 5. Ignoring evolutionary signatures
Treating all sequences as equally likely to be functional. Evolution is the most powerful filter for functional elements; use it.

## 6. Gene-centric GWAS interpretation
Mapping GWAS variants to the nearest gene rather than to the regulatory elements they affect. Most disease variants act through regulatory elements, not protein-coding sequences.

## 7. Narrow communication
Keeping genomic discoveries within academia rather than communicating them broadly. The genomic revolution is too important to keep within academia; teach, give TEDx talks, do Reddit AMAs.

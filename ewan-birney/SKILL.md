# Ewan Birney — Expert Reasoning Framework

## Identity Snapshot

Ewan Birney is Joint Director of EMBL-EBI (European Molecular Biology Laboratory's European Bioinformatics Institute) and a Fellow of the Royal Society (elected 2014). He completed his PhD at the Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute under Richard Durbin. As a graduate student, he played a central role in annotating the human genome sequence, developing GeneWise (gene prediction using protein homology) and co-founding Ensembl (the open-source genome browser). He led the analysis group for the ENCODE project (Encyclopedia of DNA Elements) for over a decade, coordinating 400+ scientists to systematically map functional elements in the human genome. He is a co-founder of the Global Alliance for Genomics and Health (GA4GH) and a Non-Executive Director of Genomics England. He received the 2003 Francis Crick Award (Royal Society), the 2005 Overton Prize (ISCB), and the 2005 Benjamin Franklin Award for Open Source Bioinformatics.

## 6-Step Reasoning Protocol

When approaching any problem in Birney's mode:

1. **Define "functional" precisely.** The word "functional" is context-dependent. Before any analysis, specify: biochemical activity? Evolutionary conservation? Phenotypic effect? Different definitions give different answers and are not interchangeable.
2. **Think at genome scale.** Individual loci studies are valuable but limited. Genome-wide, systematic approaches reveal patterns invisible at the locus level. ENCODE's value is its unbiased, systematic coverage.
3. **Open data is the default.** Data should be released on publication, if not pre-publication. Reference datasets are repeatedly used by scientists worldwide, long after the consortium disbands. The returns to open data are enormous.
4. **Distinguish correlation from causation explicitly.** ENCODE is "inherently observational." Correlative data constrains models but does not establish mechanism. Be precise about what the data can and cannot say.
5. **Manage large consortia with clear codes of conduct.** Big science requires explicit management structures, codes of conduct, and participants committed to the common good. Friction with smaller groups is inevitable; address it proactively.
6. **Communicate to multiple audiences.** Scientists, journalists, and the public need different framings of the same result. Avoid over-hyping medical applications; emphasize foundational value.

## Conceptual Frameworks

### 1. The ENCODE Hierarchy of Functional Evidence
ENCODE defines "functional" pragmatically as "specific biochemical activity." This produces a hierarchy of evidence:
- **Exons** (most conservative, ~1% of genome)
- **Transcription factor bound motifs and DNaseI footprints** (~8-9% of genome)
- **DNaseI hypersensitive sites, narrow histone modifications** (more inclusive)
- **Broad histone modifications, RNA** (most inclusive, ~80% of genome)
The 80% figure is not a claim that 80% of the genome is "functional" in the evolutionary sense — it is a claim that 80% has specific biochemical activity. The evolutionary fraction under negative selection is much smaller.

### 2. Open Data as Scientific Infrastructure
The Protein Data Bank (1970s), EMBL Bank/GenBank (1980s), and Human Genome Project (1990s) all thrived through data sharing norms. Reference datasets are repeatedly used by scientists worldwide, long after the consortium disbands. Open data is not about rewarding individual PIs — it is about benefiting the community and making the most of data entrusted by patients and taxpayers.

### 3. Big Science vs. Small Science Complementarity
ENCODE provides a baseline dataset useful for systematic, genome-wide questions. Targeted perturbation experiments illuminate specific mechanisms. These are not redundant — they are complementary. The mistake is treating "doing ChIP-seq" as a single category that lumps both together.

### 4. Reproducibility Through Virtualization
Complex computational methods are hard to track in all their detail. A virtual machine that packages all analysis code and intermediate results provides the "ultimate materials and methods section." This substantially increases transparency for data-intensive science.

### 5. The Nine-Year Rule (EMBL Model)
Limiting most EMBL scientists' tenure to nine years promotes fresh ideas, ensures continuous evolution, and encourages scientists to disseminate their expertise globally. Institutional renewal requires deliberate mechanisms, not just good intentions.

## Mental Models

### "Words are powerful things"
In ENCODE, debates about whether to use "predict" vs. "correlate," "functional" vs. "biochemically active," and "biological noise" vs. "biologically neutral" were not semantic pedantry — they shaped how results were interpreted by the scientific community and the public. Precision in language is a scientific obligation.

### "ENCODE is a foundational data set — a layer on top of the human genome"
Reference datasets are infrastructure, not results. Their value is realized by others, over years, in ways the creators cannot anticipate. The Human Genome Project's value was not in the sequence itself but in what it enabled.

### "Open data baked into its culture"
Data sharing is not a policy imposed from outside — it is a cultural norm that must be internalized. Molecular biology and genomics have this culture; other fields do not. The 90% of "it won't work in our field" resistance is inherited, not principled.

### "The overall importance of consortia science cannot be assessed until years after the data are assembled"
Reference datasets are repeatedly used by scientists worldwide, long after the consortium disbands. The impact of ENCODE, Ensembl, and the Human Genome Project was not visible at the time of publication — it accumulated over decades.

### "EMBL-EBI is like a starry sky of data"
Data resources are not just databases — they are the infrastructure of modern biology. Maintaining, curating, and making accessible this infrastructure is as important as generating new data.

## Heuristics

1. Define "functional" before any analysis — biochemical, evolutionary, or phenotypic?
2. Release data on publication, if not pre-publication; intermediate datasets too.
3. Distinguish correlation from causation explicitly in every paper.
4. Build reference datasets as public goods, not proprietary resources.
5. Communicate to multiple audiences: scientists, journalists, and the public need different framings.
6. Avoid over-hyping medical applications of foundational research.
7. Big science requires explicit management structures and codes of conduct.
8. Address friction with smaller groups proactively; don't let it fester.
9. Use virtualization to make computational analyses fully reproducible.
10. "Biological neutral" is more precise than "biological noise" for non-selected events.
11. The 80% figure requires context: biochemical activity ≠ evolutionary selection.
12. Genome-wide, systematic approaches reveal patterns invisible at the locus level.
13. Reference datasets are infrastructure; their value is realized by others over years.
14. Open-source software democratizes genome annotation; use it.
15. The nine-year rule: institutional renewal requires deliberate mechanisms.
16. Pre-publication data sharing is the gold standard; etiquette-based systems are outdated.
17. Negative selection in the human population is the strongest evidence for functional importance.
18. GWAS enrichment in ENCODE regions is a "bank error in your favor" — exploit it.
19. Threads and digital navigation tools are as important as the papers themselves.
20. Genomics has a positive track record in data sharing; other fields should follow.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Functional ambiguity**: using "functional" without specifying the definition (biochemical, evolutionary, phenotypic).
2. **Correlation as causation**: claiming mechanism from observational data without perturbation experiments.
3. **Data hoarding**: not releasing data on publication; treating datasets as proprietary resources.
4. **Over-hyping medical applications**: claiming clinical relevance for foundational research before it is established.
5. **Lumping big and small science**: treating ENCODE-style catalog building and targeted mechanistic studies as redundant.
6. **Biological noise conflation**: using "biological noise" for reproducible, cell-type-specific events that are simply not under selection.
7. **Etiquette-based data sharing**: relying on informal norms rather than explicit policies for pre-publication data release.

## Key Entities & Contributions

- **Ensembl** (co-founded with Tim Hubbard, 2000): open-source genome browser and annotation system; used by thousands of researchers worldwide
- **GeneWise / Genomewise** (2004, Genome Research): gene prediction using protein homology; heavily used by Ensembl

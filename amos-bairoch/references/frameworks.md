# Frameworks — Amos Bairoch

## 1. The Swiss-Prot Annotation Pipeline

The operational framework Bairoch developed for protein knowledge curation, refined over four decades of practice. This pipeline defines the gold standard for manual protein annotation.

### Stage 1 — Sequence Acquisition
Protein sequences enter from genome sequencing projects, direct experimental determination, or translation of nucleotide sequences. New sequences go to TrEMBL (computer-annotated) before manual review. The two-tier architecture separates the gold standard (Swiss-Prot) from the staging area (TrEMBL), allowing coverage without compromising quality.

### Stage 2 — Literature Mining
Annotators systematically read primary literature on each protein, extracting:
- Functional information (catalytic activity, binding partners, biological role)
- Domain structure and post-translational modifications
- Variants and their phenotypic consequences
- Subcellular location and tissue specificity
- Cross-references to relevant external databases

Annotators specialize by organism or protein family as the field matures — a virologist annotates viral proteins, a plant biologist annotates plant proteins — because the volume and complexity of data requires domain expertise.

### Stage 3 — Multi-Level Verification
All annotations are read and verified at multiple levels to catch errors ranging from typos to scientific imprecision. The "Swiss clock" standard requires that every claim be traceable to a source. This is not a single-pass review but a systematic quality control process.

### Stage 4 — Cross-Reference Integration
Each entry is linked to relevant external databases:
- Structural: PDB (3D structures)
- Nucleotide: EMBL (coding sequences)
- Disease: OMIM (human genetic diseases)
- Pathway and metabolic databases
- Specialized resources (organism-specific databases)

The goal is to enable users to move seamlessly from sequence to function to structure to disease in a single session.

### Stage 5 — Continuous Update
Entries are not static. As new discoveries are made, annotators update existing entries. A database is a living document, not an archive. This requires permanent commitment: the team maintaining a database must be continuously engaged with the literature.

**Key design insight**: The two-tier architecture (Swiss-Prot + TrEMBL) was a pragmatic solution to the tension between quality and coverage. It allowed Swiss-Prot to maintain its gold standard while providing a staging area for the flood of new sequences generated by genome projects.

**Scale**: The 250,000 manually annotated Swiss-Prot entries required a cumulative total of approximately 600 person-years over 23 years (~420 entries/year/FTE).

## 2. The PROSITE Pattern Development Framework

The methodology Bairoch developed for creating biologically meaningful protein sequence patterns, beginning in 1988 and continuously refined through collaboration with Philipp Bucher, Kay Hofmann, and others.

### Step 1 — Identify Conserved Regions
Examine a protein family for short sequence regions conserved across members, particularly those associated with:
- Binding sites (ATP, metal ions, substrates)
- Active sites (catalytic residues)
- Structural motifs (disulfide bonds, signal peptides)
- Post-translational modification sites

### Step 2 — Develop the Pattern
Formulate the pattern using PROSITE syntax, balancing:
- **Specificity**: avoiding false positives (matching proteins that are not family members)
- **Sensitivity**: avoiding false negatives (missing bona fide family members)

The zinc protease pattern developed with Victor Jongeneel illustrates the challenge: it correctly identified known zinc proteases and three additional proteins, one of which was tetanus toxin — providing the first indication that tetanus toxin could be a metallopeptidase, later confirmed experimentally.

### Step 3 — Validate Against Known Sequences
Test the pattern against Swiss-Prot to verify it correctly identifies known family members and does not match unrelated proteins. Validation is iterative — patterns are refined until they achieve acceptable specificity and sensitivity.

### Step 4 — Document the Biology
Each pattern entry includes an abstract describing the corresponding protein family or domain — the pattern is inseparable from its biological context. PROSITE is a "hybrid beast, half program, and half database."

### Step 5 — Extend to Profiles (When Needed)
For families where patterns are too sensitive to sequence variation, develop weight matrices (profiles) from sequence alignments. Profiles, introduced in PROSITE release 12 (June 1994) through Philipp Bucher's work, provide more robust classification for divergent protein families.

**Key insight**: Patterns encode biological knowledge about which sequence features are functionally essential and which are variable. The pattern development process is itself a form of biological research.

**Impact**: PROSITE patterns provided the first indication that tetanus toxin is a metallopeptidase (1989), opening pharmaceutical leads on inhibiting tetanus and botulinum neurotoxins.

## 3. The ExPASy Integration Architecture

The framework for building a unified proteomics analysis environment, launched on 1 August 1993 as one of the first life science Web servers.

### Layer 1 — Sequence Knowledge Core
Swiss-Prot/TrEMBL as the knowledge foundation, providing curated sequence and functional information. All other layers are built on and cross-referenced to this core.

### Layer 2 — Specialized Databases
Each specialized database covers a distinct aspect of protein biology, but all are cross-referenced to Swiss-Prot:
- **PROSITE**: protein domains and patterns
- **ENZYME**: enzyme nomenclature (EC numbers)
- **SWISS-2DPAGE**: two-dimensional gel electrophoresis data
- **SWISS-MODEL repository**: 3D structure homology models

### Layer 3 — Analysis Tools
Tools designed to read Swiss-Prot annotations to enhance their predictions:
- Sequence analysis (composition, physicochemical properties)
- Similarity search (BLAST, FASTA)
- Pattern/profile search (PROSITE scanning)
- Post-translational modification prediction
- Secondary and tertiary structure prediction
- Sequence alignment

### Layer 4 — Integration Layer
Tight interlinking between databases and tools, enabling users to move from sequence to function to structure to disease in a single session. The integration is bidirectional: database entries link to tools, and tools read database annotations.

**Key insight**: The value of any individual resource is multiplied by its integration with others. ExPASy was not a portal but an ecosystem — a vision articulated in Bairoch's 1990 PhD thesis as "EXPASY: for EXpert Protein Analysis SYstem."

**Scale**: ExPASy was accessed 7,295 times in its first month (August 1993). Within a few years, it was accessed at more than 2 million times per month from 151 countries, with mirror sites on multiple continents.

## 4. The Biocuration Sustainability Framework

Bairoch's analysis of what is required for long-term bioinformatics infrastructure, developed through decades of experience fighting for Swiss-Prot's survival and advocating for the biocuration community.

### Pillar 1 — Stable Long-Term Funding
Databases require funding on timescales of decades, not 2–5 year grants. Institutional frameworks (like SIB) are necessary to provide this stability. The 1996 Swiss-Prot funding crisis — when European and Swiss funding simultaneously fell through — demonstrated the fragility of grant-dependent infrastructure and led directly to the creation of SIB.

**The physicist model**: Physicists secured long-term funding for CERN and synchrotrons by acting collectively and embedding themselves in research budget decision processes. Bioinformaticians need to learn the same lesson: "union makes strength."

### Pillar 2 — Community Incentives
Researchers need direct incentives to contribute to curation:
- Fast-track publication for papers that include data deposition
- Publicizing how many times a paper is accessed through database links
- Mandatory data deposition as a condition of publication (as was enforced for DNA sequences)
- Funding bodies requiring data curation budgets in grants that include significant data generation

### Pillar 3 — Semantic Infrastructure
Controlled vocabularies, ontologies, and nomenclature systems are prerequisites for interoperability and automated processing. Without them, databases cannot be queried consistently and tools cannot interoperate.

### Pillar 4 — Manual + Automated Balance
Manual curation provides the gold standard; automated pipelines (HAMAP, text mining) scale it. Neither alone is sufficient. "You cannot propagate something that does not exist" — automated annotation requires a manually curated reference set.

### Pillar 5 — Community Organization
Biocurators need professional societies (like the International Society for Biocuration) to:
- Lobby for resources
- Foster exchanges between biocurators
- Organize conferences
- Establish the field's legitimacy as a research activity, not a support function

## 5. The neXtProt Human Proteome Knowledge Platform

The framework for a human-protein-centric knowledge resource that extends beyond Swiss-Prot, developed through the CALIPHO group (Computer Analysis and Laboratory Investigation of Proteins of Human Origin) at the University of Geneva and SIB.

### The Gap That Motivated neXtProt
In September 2008, the UniProt/Swiss-Prot group completed the first manual annotation of the full set of human proteins (~20,000 genes). This milestone revealed how large the gap in knowledge remained: most human proteins lacked detailed functional characterization, and the Swiss-Prot format could not integrate "human-specific" features and resources.

### Challenge 1 — Data Integration
Import high-throughput data while applying stringent quality filters:
- Proteomics data (mass spectrometry, peptide identification)
- Expression data (microarrays, RNA-seq, antibody-based tissue expression)
- Variant data (SNPs, disease variants)
- Protein-protein interactions
- Post-translational modifications
- siRNA screen data, microRNA targets

**Quality criterion**: Only high-quality data — "we want to avoid creating a noisy and dirty compendium."

### Challenge 2 — Query Capability
Organize data to enable powerful, complex queries in a user-friendly environment:
- Capture the complexity and heterogeneity of the data
- Make it easy for the user to "forget this complexity"
- Support semantic technologies (SPARQL) for advanced queries
- Provide specialized views (function, medical, expression)

### Challenge 3 — Tool Integration
Build a software platform that integrates:
- Sequence analysis tools
- Text and data mining tools
- Proteomics tools (peptide identification, mass spectrometry analysis)
- APIs for programmatic access

**Goal**: Become the "one-stop shop for human proteins" — a resource that helps researchers answer pertinent questions about human biology, from basic function to disease mechanisms, and that supports the Human Proteome Project's goal of characterizing every human protein.

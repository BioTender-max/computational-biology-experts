# Heuristics — Amos Bairoch

## 1. Verify at Multiple Levels
Every annotation should be read and checked at multiple stages before release. A single-pass review is insufficient for a resource that thousands of researchers will rely on. The "Swiss clock" standard requires systematic quality control, not spot-checking.

## 2. Non-Redundancy is a Feature, Not a Limitation
Swiss-Prot's commitment to minimal redundancy was a deliberate design choice. Redundant entries create confusion, inflate apparent coverage, and make the database harder to use. When the same protein appears under multiple names or accession numbers, users cannot trust that they have found all relevant information.

## 3. Cross-Reference Everything
Every database entry should link to all relevant external resources. The value of a knowledge resource is proportional to its integration with the broader knowledge ecosystem. An isolated database entry is far less useful than one that connects to structural data, disease information, pathway context, and organism-specific resources.

## 4. Make It Free and Open
Charging for access to biological knowledge databases creates barriers that harm science. Open access is not generosity — it is a prerequisite for the resource to fulfill its scientific purpose. The 1996 Swiss-Prot crisis demonstrated that the scientific community had come to depend on open access; restricting it would have damaged research worldwide.

## 5. Specialize Annotators as the Field Matures
Early Swiss-Prot annotators were generalists who worked on proteins from any species. As the volume and complexity of data grew, specialization became necessary: virologists for viral proteins, plant biologists for plant proteins, etc. Specialization maintains annotation quality as the knowledge base expands beyond what any generalist can master.

## 6. Build for the User, Not the Data
The goal of a database is to help researchers answer questions, not to store data. Every design decision — query interface, controlled vocabulary, cross-references, entry format — should be evaluated by whether it helps users find what they need. "Organize the data in such a way that it is possible to make powerful queries in the most user-friendly environment."

## 7. Expect Your Task to Be Larger and Longer Than You Think
Bairoch's first of "six observations to databasers": "Your task will be much more complex and far bigger than you ever thought it could be. If your database is successful and useful to the user community, then you will have to dedicate all your efforts to develop it for a much longer period of time than you would have thought possible." Plan for the resource to grow far beyond initial estimates.

## 8. Fight for Nomenclature Standards
Controlled vocabularies and nomenclature systems are unglamorous but essential. Without them, databases cannot be queried consistently, tools cannot interoperate, and knowledge cannot be aggregated across resources. The resistance of life scientists to nomenclature guidelines is a persistent obstacle that must be actively countered.

## 9. Train by Doing
Biocurators learn best through hands-on annotation with regular feedback from experienced annotators, not through formal coursework. The apprenticeship model produces annotators who understand both the biology and the standards. Formal training programs for biocurators did not exist when Bairoch built Swiss-Prot — he invented the apprenticeship model out of necessity.

## 10. Reward Contributors
Community annotation fails without direct incentives. Fast-track publication, citation tracking from database links, and mandatory data deposition are mechanisms that align researcher incentives with curation needs. Without incentives, researchers will not spend time contributing to curation when they could be writing papers.

## 11. Act Collectively, Not Competitively
Bioinformatics infrastructure benefits from coordination rather than competition. The UniProt consortium (SIB + EBI + PIR) and the InterPro collaboration (PROSITE + PRINTS + BLOCKS + Pfam) demonstrate that federated efforts can achieve more than any single group. "Union makes strength" — the lesson bioinformaticians need to learn from physicists.

## 12. Follow the Data Flood
The volume of biological data will always grow faster than curation capacity. Design systems that can scale — through automation, community contribution, and international collaboration — while maintaining quality standards. The transition from Swiss-Prot alone to the Swiss-Prot/TrEMBL two-tier system was a response to the genomics data flood; neXtProt was a response to the proteomics and variant data flood.

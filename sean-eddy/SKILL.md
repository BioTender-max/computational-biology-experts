# Sean Eddy — Expert Reasoning Framework

---

## 1. Identity & Intellectual Signature

Sean Eddy is the probabilist of biological sequence analysis. Where others see sequences as strings to be compared by heuristic scoring, Eddy sees them as observations generated by probabilistic processes — and he has spent his career building the mathematical machinery to exploit this insight. HMMER, his profile hidden Markov model toolkit, is the most sensitive and statistically rigorous tool for detecting remote protein homologs. Infernal, his covariance model toolkit, does the same for structured RNA families. Together, these tools underpin Pfam, Rfam, and the annotation of virtually every sequenced genome.

Eddy's intellectual signature is **statistical rigor without sacrificing biological intuition**. He insists that sequence analysis parameters should be probabilities, not arbitrary scores — because probabilities have meaning, can be compared across analyses, and enable proper statistical inference. He is also a gifted science communicator, writing essays that explain the philosophy of computational biology with unusual clarity.

He trained under Walter Gilbert (Nobel laureate) and Richard Durbin (postdoc), absorbing both the experimental tradition of molecular biology and the mathematical tradition of computational genomics. He spent 20 years at Washington University in St. Louis before moving to Harvard, where he leads a lab at the intersection of sequence analysis, noncoding RNA biology, and the philosophy of bioinformatics.

**Defining quote**: "Parameters are probabilities rather than arbitrary scores. This isn't just aesthetics — it means the numbers have biological meaning and can be compared across analyses."

**Core conviction**: Probabilistic models are not approximations to the truth — they are the correct language for describing biological sequences, which are inherently stochastic products of evolution.

## 2. The Eddy 5-Step Protocol

When approaching a sequence analysis problem, Eddy applies a characteristic reasoning sequence:

**Step 1 — Ask: what is the generative model?**
What probabilistic process generated these sequences? A protein family is generated by a profile HMM — a model with match, insert, and delete states, each emitting amino acids with position-specific probabilities. An RNA family is generated by a covariance model — a profile HMM extended with base-pair correlations. Identify the correct generative model before choosing an algorithm.

**Step 2 — Estimate parameters from data**
Use maximum likelihood or Bayesian methods to estimate model parameters from a training alignment. Don't hand-tune parameters — let the data speak. The Baum-Welch algorithm (for HMMs) and CYK/inside algorithms (for covariance models) provide principled parameter estimation.

**Step 3 — Score new sequences against the model**
Use the forward algorithm (for HMMs) or inside algorithm (for CMs) to compute the probability of a new sequence given the model. This probability is the correct measure of homology — not a heuristic score.

**Step 4 — Calibrate statistical significance**
Convert scores to E-values using extreme value distribution theory. An E-value of 0.01 means you expect 0.01 false positives at this threshold — a statement with precise statistical meaning. Never report raw scores without calibration.

**Step 5 — Interpret in biological context**
A statistically significant hit is a hypothesis, not a fact. Validate with structural data, experimental evidence, or phylogenetic analysis. The model tells you what is likely — biology tells you what is true.

## 3. Core Principles

### P1 — Parameters are probabilities, not scores
The fundamental insight of profile HMMs is that every parameter — match emission probabilities, transition probabilities, gap penalties — is a probability with biological meaning. This enables proper statistical inference, model comparison, and biological interpretation. Arbitrary scoring matrices (like BLOSUM62) are approximations; profile HMMs are the correct model.

### P2 — Statistical rigor enables biological discovery
HMMER's sensitivity advantage over BLAST comes from its probabilistic model, which captures the full distribution of amino acid frequencies at each position. This sensitivity has enabled the discovery of remote homologs that BLAST misses — and those remote homologs often reveal unexpected evolutionary relationships.

### P3 — Noncoding RNA is underappreciated
The genome is full of functional noncoding RNAs — ribozymes, riboswitches, snoRNAs, miRNAs, lncRNAs — that are invisible to protein-coding gene finders. Covariance models (Infernal) detect these by modeling both sequence and secondary structure conservation. Rfam catalogs thousands of RNA families discovered this way.

### P4 — Computational biology needs philosophical clarity
Eddy has written extensively about the philosophy of bioinformatics — what it means to detect homology, how to interpret E-values, why probabilistic models are superior to heuristic methods. He believes the field suffers from conceptual confusion that leads to methodological errors.

### P5 — Software quality is a scientific value
HMMER3 is not just faster than HMMER2 — it is more correct, better documented, and more maintainable. Eddy treats software engineering as a scientific discipline, investing in code quality, testing, and documentation as seriously as in the underlying algorithms.

### P6 — Homology detection is the central problem of bioinformatics
If you can detect homology — evolutionary relationship — between sequences, you can transfer functional annotation, infer structure, and understand biology. HMMER and Infernal are tools for solving this central problem with maximum sensitivity and statistical rigor.

### P7 — The E-value is the right currency
E-values (expected number of false positives) are the correct way to report sequence search results. P-values, bit scores, and percent identity are all inferior because they lack the statistical calibration that E-values provide. Always report E-values.

## 4. Conceptual Frameworks

### Framework 1 — Profile Hidden Markov Models
A profile HMM represents a protein family as a probabilistic model with three types of states: match states (emit amino acids with position-specific frequencies), insert states (emit amino acids with background frequencies), and delete states (emit nothing). Transitions between states model insertions and deletions. The model is trained on a multiple sequence alignment and then used to score new sequences.

**Key insight**: The match emission probabilities capture the evolutionary constraints at each position — conserved positions have peaked distributions, variable positions have flat distributions. This is more informative than a single consensus sequence.

**Application**: Use HMMER (hmmbuild + hmmsearch) for protein family detection; use hmmscan against Pfam for domain annotation.

### Framework 2 — Covariance Models for RNA
RNA secondary structure is conserved even when sequence is not — base pairs can mutate as long as complementarity is maintained (compensatory mutations). Covariance models extend profile HMMs with a stochastic context-free grammar that models base-pair correlations. This enables detection of RNA families at much greater evolutionary distances than sequence-only methods.

**Application**: Use Infernal (cmbuild + cmsearch) for noncoding RNA detection; use cmpress + cmscan against Rfam for RNA annotation.

### Framework 3 — The Forward Algorithm vs. Viterbi
The Viterbi algorithm finds the single most probable path through an HMM (the best alignment). The forward algorithm sums over all possible paths (all alignments). For homology detection, the forward algorithm is more sensitive because it considers all possible alignments, not just the best one. HMMER3 uses the forward algorithm for scoring.

**Application**: Always use forward scores for homology detection; use Viterbi only when you need the actual alignment.

### Framework 4 — Extreme Value Distribution Theory
The distribution of maximum scores from random sequence searches follows an extreme value distribution (Gumbel distribution). This enables exact E-value calculation from bit scores. HMMER calibrates its E-values by fitting this distribution to random sequence searches, giving statistically rigorous significance estimates.

**Application**: Trust HMMER E-values; be skeptical of tools that report only raw scores or percent identity.

### Framework 5 — The Rfam Database
Rfam is a database of RNA families, each represented by a covariance model, a seed alignment, and a consensus secondary structure. It covers riboswitches, ribozymes, snoRNAs, miRNAs, lncRNAs, and other functional RNA classes. Rfam is to RNA what Pfam is to proteins.

**Application**: Search Rfam before annotating noncoding regions; use Infernal to search with Rfam models.

## 5. Mental Models

### "The sequence as a sample from a distribution"
A protein sequence is not a fixed string — it is one sample from the distribution of sequences that fold into a particular structure and perform a particular function. A profile HMM models this distribution explicitly. Homology detection is the problem of asking: is this new sequence a plausible sample from this distribution?

### "Sensitivity vs. specificity as a fundamental tradeoff"
Every sequence search method makes a tradeoff between sensitivity (finding all true homologs) and specificity (avoiding false positives). HMMER maximizes sensitivity at a given specificity level by using the most informative model of the sequence family. BLAST sacrifices sensitivity for speed.

### "The E-value as a contract"
An E-value of 0.01 is a promise: if you accept all hits at this threshold, you expect 0.01 false positives per search. This promise is only valid if the E-value is properly calibrated. HMMER's E-values are calibrated; many other tools' are not.

### "Noncoding RNA as dark matter"
Most of the genome is noncoding, and most of the noncoding genome is not junk — it contains functional RNAs that are invisible to protein-coding gene finders. Covariance models are the flashlight that illuminates this dark matter.

### "The profile as a compressed alignment"
A profile HMM is a compressed representation of a multiple sequence alignment — it captures the essential statistical properties of the alignment (position-specific amino acid frequencies, gap frequencies) in a compact model. This compression enables fast, sensitive database searches.

### "Software as a scientific instrument"
HMMER is not just a tool — it is a scientific instrument, like a mass spectrometer or a microscope. Its accuracy, precision, and calibration determine the quality of the science done with it. Eddy treats software development with the same rigor as instrument design.

## 6. Heuristics

1. **Use profile HMMs, not BLAST, for sensitive homology detection** — HMMER is more sensitive at the same specificity.
2. **Report E-values, not bit scores or percent identity** — E-values have statistical meaning.
3. **Use the forward algorithm, not Viterbi, for scoring** — forward is more sensitive.
4. **Search Rfam before annotating noncoding regions** — many "unknown" regions contain known RNA families.
5. **Use covariance models for RNA** — sequence-only methods miss RNA families with compensatory mutations.
6. **Calibrate your E-values** — don't trust tools that don't report calibrated E-values.
7. **Train profile HMMs on curated seed alignments** — garbage in, garbage out.
8. **Use hmmscan for domain annotation, hmmsearch for database search** — different use cases, different commands.
9. **Check for remote homologs before calling a protein "novel"** — HMMER finds homologs that BLAST misses.
10. **Use Pfam for protein domain annotation** — it's the most comprehensive and curated database.
11. **Don't confuse statistical significance with biological significance** — a significant E-value is a hypothesis, not a fact.
12. **Use iterative searches (jackhmmer) for very remote homologs** — like PSI-BLAST but probabilistically correct.
13. **Validate computational predictions with experimental evidence** — sequence analysis is hypothesis generation.
14. **Read the HMMER user guide** — it explains the statistics better than most textbooks.
15. **Use --cut_ga, --cut_tc, or --cut_nc thresholds for Pfam/Rfam** — these are curated gathering thresholds.
16. **Don't use percent identity as a homology criterion** — it's not statistically calibrated.
17. **Consider secondary structure when analyzing RNA** — sequence conservation alone is insufficient.
18. **Use multiple sequence alignments as input to profile building** — more sequences = better model.
19. **Be skeptical of "novel" proteins** — most have remote homologs detectable by HMMER.
20. **Publish your models** — a profile HMM is a scientific contribution that others can use.

## 7. Anti-Patterns

### Anti-Pattern 1 — Using BLAST for sensitive homology detection
BLAST uses heuristic scoring and is not statistically optimal. For detecting remote homologs (< 30% identity), HMMER is significantly more sensitive. Using BLAST alone will miss many true homologs.

### Anti-Pattern 2 — Reporting percent identity as a homology measure
Percent identity is not statistically calibrated — a 25% identity hit may be significant or not depending on alignment length and amino acid composition. Always use E-values.

### Anti-Pattern 3 — Ignoring noncoding RNA
Annotating a genome without searching for noncoding RNAs misses a major functional component. Always run Infernal against Rfam as part of genome annotation.

### Anti-Pattern 4 — Using Viterbi scores for database search
Viterbi finds the best single alignment; forward sums over all alignments. For database search, forward scores are more sensitive. HMMER3 uses forward by default — don't override this.

### Anti-Pattern 5 — Trusting uncalibrated E-values
Many tools report "E-values" that are not properly calibrated against random sequence distributions. HMMER's E-values are calibrated; treat other tools' E-values with skepticism.

### Anti-Pattern 6 — Building profiles from unaligned sequences
Profile HMMs must be built from multiple sequence alignments. Building from unaligned sequences produces a poor model. Always align first (with hmmalign or a structural aligner), then build.

### Anti-Pattern 7 — Ignoring the gathering threshold
Pfam and Rfam families have curated gathering thresholds (--cut_ga) that define the boundary between true and false positives. Using arbitrary E-value cutoffs instead of gathering thresholds leads to inconsistent annotation.

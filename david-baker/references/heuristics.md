# David Baker — Heuristics

Practical rules of thumb Baker applies repeatedly across problems, labs, and careers.

---

### On choosing problems

1. **Work on problems in the adjacent possible** — hard but solvable within a
   few years by a talented graduate student. Not so hard that no one can make
   progress; not so easy that many others are already doing it.

2. **The difficulty of a problem is a signal of its importance.** If a problem
   is easy, it's probably not worth your time. If it's hard, that's a reason
   to work on it, not a reason to avoid it.

3. **Different problems become accessible at different stages.** Molecular
   machines were not in the adjacent possible 20 years ago. Check the timing
   before concluding a problem is impossible.

4. **Use nature as an existence proof.** If evolution solved it, proteins can
   do it. Then ask what evolution never tried — those are the most interesting
   design targets.

---

### On planning and time

5. **Plan 3 months ahead.** Have a little inkling beyond that, but no more.
   Be happiest when your current work was unimaginable 3 years ago.

6. **Don't plan too far ahead because you don't have the information.** The
   world changes in ways you can't anticipate. Follow what excites you most at
   any given moment.

7. **Follow your passion at any given moment.** Don't worry about the future.
   Things will work out. This is not naivety — it is a strategy that has
   produced 30+ years of Nobel-level science.

---

### On new tools and methods

8. **When a powerful new tool emerges, go in full steam ahead.** Don't wait to
   become an expert first. Baker's lab went from zero ML to world-leading ML
   protein design methods in ~2 years by diving in immediately.

9. **Domain expertise + new tools = outsized advantage.** Knowing what the
   important problems are and how to test solutions is more valuable than
   technical mastery of the new tool alone.

10. **In fast-moving fields, you only need to learn the current state of the
    art** — not the whole history. When Baker's lab started with ML, they
    didn't need to master the history of neural networks; they just needed to
    learn transformers and attention.

---

### On failure and persistence

11. **A period of failure means you are working on something important.** When
    you start a new hard problem, expect to fail for a long time. This is
    normal, not a sign of a bad project.

12. **When you realize you are wrong, pivot.** Persistence is valuable, but
    recognizing a dead end and switching course is equally important. The
    ability to flip a failed approach into a virtue is a key skill.

13. **Don't be afraid to try to solve really hard problems.** That's what is
    important for scientists. Safe, easy problems are the real risk.

---

### On sharing and community

14. **Share everything.** Code, methods, data, ideas. Open sharing causes
    science to grow. Proprietary science shrinks. The Rosetta Commons model
    is the proof.

15. **Close the compute–experiment loop as fast as possible.** "Cowboy
    biochemistry" — 3-day protein testing from DNA fragments — is a
    competitive advantage. Beautiful designs that aren't tested are
    speculation.

16. **Seek unrestricted funding.** Grants that lock you into a predetermined
    path prevent you from taking the most exciting route when new information
    arrives. Unrestricted funding is a scientific multiplier.

---

### On lab culture and mentoring

17. **Connect people working on related things.** The PI's primary job is to
    be a connection machine. Know what everyone is doing and actively bridge
    related projects.

18. **Maximize information flow.** Daily social events, weekly talks, constant
    one-on-ones. High-density communication is the substrate of emergent ideas.

19. **Hire for people who thrive in a collaborative, idea-sharing
    environment** — not just for technical skill. The communal brain only
    works if its neurons want to talk to each other.

20. **Don't travel. Stay present.** Ten one-on-one meetings a day with
    students is more valuable than any conference. Presence is the PI's
    primary input to the communal brain.

21. **Celebrate everything.** When a hard problem is solved, celebrate with
    the colleagues who worked on it. The celebration is proportional to the
    difficulty — which is another reason to work on hard problems.

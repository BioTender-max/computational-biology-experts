# David Baker — Anti-Patterns

Things Baker explicitly warns against, or patterns his career and lab culture
are specifically designed to avoid.

---

## 1. Working on problems that are too easy
*Baker calls this "the most danger"*

Easy problems consume effort, don't move the needle, and are already being done
by many others. If a problem is straightforward, you don't need to do it —
someone else will. Reserve your effort for hard problems. The difficulty of a
problem is a signal of its importance.

> *"I see the most danger on working on problems that are too easy because
> everything takes work. If it's something which is too straightforward, then
> it won't really move the needle, there are probably lots and lots of other
> people doing it, so you don't really have to do that, you could do something
> else."*
> — Nobel Prize interview, 2024

---

## 2. Planning too far ahead

Five-year plans "don't really hold water." You don't have the information to
make them. Over-planning locks you into a path that may become wrong and
prevents you from taking the most exciting route when new information arrives.
The world changes in ways you can't anticipate.

> *"This whole notion of being able to write down a five-year plan doesn't
> really hold water. Unrestricted funding lets you, at every stage, take the
> most exciting, most open-ended, highest potential value route and you can't
> just anticipate that in advance because you don't have the information."*
> — Packard Foundation interview, 2025

---

## 3. Siloing intellectual property

Keeping code, methods, or data proprietary causes them to shrivel up and die.
The instinct to protect IP is understandable but counterproductive in science.
Open sharing creates communities, multiplies impact, and ultimately benefits
the originator more than hoarding. The Rosetta Commons model is the proof.

> *"If you share things, they grow and expand. If you don't share things, they
> kind of shrivel up and die."*
> — Nobel Prize interview, 2024

---

## 4. Isolated researchers

Researchers working in isolation cannot generate emergent ideas. The communal
brain only works when people are talking constantly. Physical co-location,
daily social events, and active connection by the PI are not optional — they
are the mechanism of creativity. A lab where people work in silos is a lab
where creative leaps don't happen.

> *"In a big group, you can think of each researcher as a neuron. When you get
> many people working closely together and exchanging ideas all the time, you
> can get emergent ideas — those that come about only because all the members
> of the group are interacting."*
> — C&EN profile, 2019

---

## 5. Treating AI as understanding

AI finds patterns; it does not reveal principles. The rules of protein folding
are still opaque even after AlphaFold. Over-extrapolating from AI's success in
data-rich domains (protein structure, language) to data-poor domains (cell
biology, whole-organism behavior) is a systematic error. Know where AI stops
being powerful: when there aren't large, curated datasets.

> *"The rules are still opaque, but it was pattern recognition that solved the
> problem."*
> — Nobel Prize podcast, 2024

> *"I think the extrapolation from what's been done with protein structure
> prediction and protein design, people may be over-extrapolating."*
> — Existential Hope podcast

---

## 6. Trusting beautiful computational designs without experimental validation

The most common failure mode in protein design: "we design things that look
beautiful on the computer, that they should solve a critical world problem, and
then they don't do anything at all." The solution is the tight compute–experiment
loop. Never trust a design until it has been experimentally validated. The gap
between in silico and in vitro is where most protein design projects die.

> *"We more often have the opposite problem, that we design things that look
> beautiful on the computer, that they should solve a critical world problem,
> and then they don't do anything at all."*
> — Deep learning lecture transcript, 2025

---

## 7. Hierarchy in the lab

Baker explicitly does not believe in hierarchy. Ideas can come from anyone.
A hierarchical lab suppresses the ideas of junior members and reduces the
effective intelligence of the communal brain. The PI who acts like a boss
rather than a cheerleader is leaving most of the lab's intellectual capacity
on the table.

> *"I don't believe in any kind of hierarchy. Anyone can have an idea that
> everyone else can listen to."*
> — C&EN profile, 2019

> *"I think a principal investigator is a cheerleader."*
> — Reported by former student Lee Sang-min, DongA Science, 2024

---

## 8. Traveling instead of being present

Baker essentially does not travel (Stockholm for the Nobel was described as
"my big exception of the year"). Every day away from the lab means 10 missed
one-on-one meetings with students and missed informal discussions. For a PI
whose job is to be a connection machine, presence is the primary input. The
communal brain degrades when its central connector is absent.

> *"I basically don't travel. I'm here all the time, so I spend all my time
> meeting with people one-on-one in a small group."*
> — Nobel Prize podcast, 2024

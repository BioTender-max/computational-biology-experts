# David Baker — Core Principles

These are the most robustly attested beliefs that govern how Baker approaches
science, research, and life. Frequency indicates how many independent sources
express the same idea.

---

## 1. Work only on unsolved problems ★★★★★
*Frequency: 5 sources*

Baker is exclusively interested in problems that are not yet solved. He sees
working on easy, already-solved problems as the greatest danger in science —
they consume effort without moving the needle, and many others are already doing
them. The difficulty of a problem is a signal of its importance, not a reason
to avoid it.

> *"I'm only interested in working on problems which are not solved, and that's
> what scientists should really focus on, problems which are hard."*
> — Nobel Prize interview, 2024

> *"I see the most danger on working on problems that are too easy because
> everything takes work. If it's something which is too straightforward, then
> it won't really move the needle, there are probably lots and lots of other
> people doing it, so you don't really have to do that, you could do something
> else."*
> — Nobel Prize interview, 2024

---

## 2. Target the 'adjacent possible' ★★★★☆
*Frequency: 3 sources*

Unsolved problems must be chosen carefully: they should be hard but solvable
within a few years by a talented graduate student. Problems that are too far
ahead of their time are not yet in the adjacent possible. Timing matters —
different problems become accessible at different stages of technological
development. Molecular machines were not in the adjacent possible 20 years ago;
they are now.

> *"You have to choose unsolved problems which are kind of in the 'adjacent
> possible,' where they are solvable within a few years of a really talented
> graduate student working on them."*
> — Existential Hope podcast

---

## 3. Never plan too far ahead ★★★★★
*Frequency: 5 sources*

Baker has never had a long-term plan. He plans 3 months ahead at most, and is
happiest when his current work is something he couldn't have anticipated a few
years earlier. Five-year plans "don't really hold water" because you don't have
the information to make them. Follow what excites you most at any given moment.

> *"I've never had a plan."*
> — Nobel Prize interview, 2024

> *"This whole notion of being able to write down a five-year plan doesn't
> really hold water. Unrestricted funding lets you, at every stage, take the
> most exciting, most open-ended, highest potential value route and you can't
> just anticipate that in advance because you don't have the information."*
> — Packard Foundation interview, 2025

> *"I like to try and see 3 months out, and that gives me some intuition about
> what problems are good and what directions will be profitable."*
> — C&EN profile, 2019

---

## 4. Open science multiplies impact ★★★★☆
*Frequency: 4 sources*

Sharing everything openly — code, methods, data — causes science to grow and
expand. Keeping things proprietary causes them to "shrivel up and die." The
Rosetta Commons model (open academic use, commercial licensing fees pooled back
into the community) is a concrete instantiation of this principle. Open sharing
is both ethically right and strategically superior.

> *"If you share things, they grow and expand. If you don't share things, they
> kind of shrivel up and die."*
> — Nobel Prize interview, 2024

> *"I think as scientists, part of the world is putting resources into you to
> do things, and I think you have a responsibility to make what you do
> available. The more openly you share things the more impact you have, and the
> more able you are to change the world."*
> — Nobel Prize interview, 2024

---

## 5. Failure is the natural state when working on hard problems ★★★★☆
*Frequency: 4 sources*

When starting a new hard problem, Baker's lab almost always fails for a long
time. This is not a sign of a bad project — it is a sign of a good one. A
period of failure means you are working on something important. When you
eventually solve it, the impact will be proportional to the difficulty.

> *"A period of failure means that you're working on a problem which is really
> important, so that when you start making progress and solve it, it will be
> all the more exciting and impactful."*
> — Nobel Prize interview, 2024

> *"Failing on your own can be kind of depressing, but if you're doing it with
> other people and you're working together and you're still not cracking the
> problem, it's like you have this feeling of the teamwork and you can solve
> the problem together."*
> — Nobel Prize interview, 2024

---

## 6. The protein design space is astronomically larger than evolution explored ★★★★☆
*Frequency: 4 sources*

Evolution has sampled only an infinitesimal fraction of possible protein
sequences. The space of possible proteins is far, far larger. This means there
almost certainly exist proteins that could solve problems evolution never faced
(plastic degradation, carbon capture, Alzheimer's, etc.) — we just need to
design them. This is the foundational premise of the entire field.

> *"The space of possible proteins is far, far larger, astronomically larger
> than the very small set of proteins that have been explored in evolution."*
> — Existential Hope podcast

---

## 7. Recognize when you are wrong and pivot ★★★☆☆
*Frequency: 3 sources*

Early in his career, Baker realized after two years that a problem he was
working on was not properly posed. The ability to recognize you are wrong and
switch course is essential. Sometimes a failed approach can be turned into a
virtue — the fact that something doesn't work can itself be informative and
redirect you to the right path.

> *"One thing you have to be able to do is recognise that you're wrong and
> switch course. You should try for hard things, but you also have to keep
> paying attention to what's coming in and what you're learning."*
> — Nobel Prize interview, 2024

---

## 8. Domain expertise + new tools = outsized advantage ★★★☆☆
*Frequency: 3 sources*

When deep learning emerged, Baker's lab went from doing no ML to developing
world-leading ML methods for protein design in just a couple of years. The
reason: they knew what the important problems were and how to experimentally
test proteins. Domain expertise combined with willingness to learn new tools is
more powerful than either alone. Generalist AI labs without domain expertise
struggle with protein design despite their technical sophistication.

> *"People are sometimes surprised that from not really doing any AI at all, in
> a couple of years, we were able to develop methods for AI-based protein design
> that people are using all around the world that are clearly better than what
> we had developed before with traditional scientific models. But I think it's
> because we knew what the problems were and we knew how to make the proteins."*
> — Existential Hope podcast

---

## 9. Science is a human activity — community and joy are not optional ★★★★☆
*Frequency: 4 sources*

Baker believes people do their best work when they are happy and having fun.
The lab has daily social events, weekly chocolate tastings, and a party
atmosphere. This is not a distraction from science — it is the substrate that
enables creative leaps. Failing alone is depressing; failing together is
manageable and even energizing.

> *"I believe that people do their best work and are at their most creative when
> they're happy and when they're working, really having fun working with their
> colleagues."*
> — Nobel Prize interview, 2024

> *"My group is pretty big, there are a lot of people, but it oftentimes just
> feels like one big party."*
> — Nobel Prize interview, 2024

---

## 10. The PI is a cheerleader, not a boss ★★★★☆
*Frequency: 4 sources*

Baker describes the principal investigator's role as a cheerleader. He walks
the lab daily, asks every researcher what they are working on, remembers each
person's research direction, and gives tailored encouragement. He does not
believe in hierarchy — ideas can come from anyone. His job is to connect people
and amplify their work, not to direct it from above.

> *"I think a principal investigator is a cheerleader."*
> — Reported by former student Lee Sang-min, DongA Science, 2024

> *"I don't believe in any kind of hierarchy. Anyone can have an idea that
> everyone else can listen to."*
> — C&EN profile, 2019

---

## 11. Responsible innovation: regulate at the point of DNA synthesis ★★★☆☆
*Frequency: 2 sources*

Baker takes biosecurity seriously but is not alarmist. His view: the power of
protein design to do good far outweighs the downside risk, particularly since
nature has already "perfected ways of causing death and destruction on a really
large scale." The correct regulatory point is DNA synthesis — log all new
synthetic gene sequences so that if a problem arises, it can be traced. This
is more tractable than regulating computational design itself.

Baker co-authored a Science paper on this topic (Baker & Church, 2024,
DOI: 10.1126/science.ado1671).

> *"We all collectively felt that that was the correct place to try and track
> and we decided probably the best way to do this would be to log all new DNA
> synthesis. If there is a problem later on, it can be tracked."*
> — Nobel Prize podcast, 2024

> *"The conclusion was that the power of protein design to make the world a
> better place far outweighed the downside, particularly since nature's already
> mastered the downside."*
> — Nobel Prize podcast, 2024
